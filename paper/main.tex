% ABSTRACT

\begin{abstract}
Accurate localisation is a fundamental requirement for the safe and reliable operation of
autonomous vehicles (AVs), yet Global Positioning System (GPS) signals remain
unreliable or entirely unavailable in urban canyons, tunnels, and densely forested areas.
This paper presents a novel LiDAR-based localisation framework that operates
independently of GPS by exploiting the rich spatial structure of three-dimensional point
cloud data. The proposed method integrates three complementary components: dynamic
Savitzky-Golay parameter adjustment for adaptive noise suppression, first- and
second-order derivative analysis of polar-transformed point clouds for environmental
feature extraction, and a weighted angular-distribution sensor position estimator.
Extensive experiments are conducted on the KITTI-CARLA benchmark dataset spanning
seven simulated environments that cover urban, rural, and highway scenarios. The
proposed approach is compared against four established baseline methods: K-Means
clustering, Convex Hull estimation, Minimum Enclosing Circle, and Iterative Closest
Point (ICP). Results demonstrate consistent improvements of 57\%--96\% in localisation
accuracy across all environments, with average positional errors as low as 2.46~m,
confirming the robustness and generalisability of the framework.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{A}{utonomous} vehicles (AVs) represent one of the most transformative
developments in modern transportation, promising to reduce road fatalities, alleviate
traffic congestion, and improve mobility for those unable to drive \cite{hussain2019autonomous}.
From simple driver-assistance features such as adaptive cruise control and lane-keeping
to the conditional automation defined at SAE Level~3 \cite{sae2021j3016}, the field has
advanced rapidly over the past decade. Central to this progress is the ability of an AV
to determine its precise position and orientation within its environment---a capability
known as \emph{localisation}. Without accurate and reliable localisation, even the most
sophisticated perception and planning algorithms cannot guarantee safe navigation.

Contemporary localisation systems rely heavily on the Global Positioning System (GPS),
which provides metre-level accuracy under open-sky conditions
\cite{luo2019localization}. However, GPS performance degrades significantly in
environments where satellite signals are obstructed or reflected, including urban
canyons formed by tall buildings, road tunnels, underground car parks, and densely
forested rural roads \cite{schreiber2016vehicle}. In these GPS-denied or GPS-degraded
scenarios, AVs must depend on onboard sensors to maintain continuous positional
awareness.

Light Detection and Ranging (LiDAR) sensors have emerged as a leading technology for
GPS-independent localisation owing to their ability to generate dense, high-resolution
three-dimensional (3D) point clouds at high frame rates \cite{kocic2018sensors}.
Unlike cameras, LiDAR is not affected by ambient lighting conditions, and unlike Radar,
it provides centimetre-level spatial resolution, making it well suited for detailed
environmental mapping and feature extraction. Despite these advantages, extracting
reliable positional estimates from raw LiDAR point clouds in diverse, unstructured
environments remains a challenging open problem.

\subsection{Limitations of Existing Approaches}

Established LiDAR localisation methods can be broadly categorised into
\emph{geometric registration} approaches and \emph{map-based matching} approaches.
Geometric registration methods such as Iterative Closest Point (ICP)
\cite{besl1992method}, Generalised-ICP (G-ICP) \cite{segal2009generalized}, and
Normal Distributions Transform (NDT) \cite{biber2003normal} align successive point
clouds to estimate relative motion. While effective in feature-rich environments, these
methods are sensitive to initial alignment quality, prone to local minima, and
computationally demanding for real-time deployment. Map-based methods \cite{levinson2010robust,wolcott2017robust} achieve high accuracy but require pre-built
high-definition (HD) maps that are costly to construct and maintain, and which quickly
become outdated in dynamic environments.

Simpler geometric estimators---including K-Means centroid clustering, Convex Hull
approximation, and Minimum Enclosing Circle fitting---offer lower computational cost
but oversimplify the spatial distribution of point cloud data. These methods frequently
fail to capture fine-grained environmental structure, leading to large positional errors
in scenarios involving tight turns, path overlaps, or varying point cloud density.

\subsection{Contributions}

This paper addresses these limitations by proposing a novel LiDAR-only localisation
framework that does not require HD maps, prior scene knowledge, or GPS assistance. The
framework processes raw LiDAR point cloud data through a three-stage pipeline that
adaptively suppresses noise, extracts meaningful environmental boundaries, and
triangulates sensor position using angular feature density. The specific contributions
of this work are as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Dynamic Savitzky-Golay Adaptive Filter:} A parameter-free smoothing
    stage that automatically selects the optimal window length and polynomial order for
    the Savitzky-Golay filter \cite{savitzky1964smoothing} based on the noise
    characteristics of each individual point cloud frame, preserving critical structural
    features while suppressing measurement noise.

  \item \textbf{Polar-Domain Derivative Analysis:} A novel application of first- and
    second-order derivatives to polar-transformed LiDAR data that identifies
    significant environmental boundaries---such as edges, corners, and surface
    discontinuities---which serve as reliable localisation anchors across diverse
    scene types.

  \item \textbf{Weighted Angular Position Estimator:} A sensor position estimation
    function that refines an initial geometric-centroid estimate by weighting
    contributions from angularly dense regions of the point cloud and further
    correcting the estimate using derivative-identified feature locations.

  \item \textbf{Comprehensive Experimental Evaluation:} Systematic evaluation on the
    KITTI-CARLA benchmark dataset across seven distinct simulated environments,
    demonstrating improvements of 57\%--96\% in localisation accuracy over four
    baseline methods: K-Means, Convex Hull, Minimum Enclosing Circle, and ICP.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

Localisation for autonomous vehicles has been studied extensively across a range of
sensor modalities. This section reviews the most relevant prior work, organised by
sensing approach, and identifies the gaps that motivate the proposed method.

% ---------------------------
\subsection{LiDAR-Based Localisation}

LiDAR sensors have been a cornerstone of autonomous vehicle research since the
DARPA Urban Challenge \cite{hussain2019autonomous}, owing to their ability to
generate dense, geometrically accurate 3D point clouds independent of lighting
conditions. The most widely adopted LiDAR localisation algorithms are built on
point-cloud registration.

\textit{Iterative Closest Point (ICP)} \cite{besl1992method} estimates the rigid
transformation between successive scans by iteratively minimising the sum of
squared distances between corresponding points. Although accurate in
feature-rich environments, ICP is susceptible to local minima, sensitive to
the quality of the initial alignment estimate, and computationally expensive
for dense point clouds. \textit{Generalised-ICP (G-ICP)} \cite{segal2009generalized}
extends ICP by modelling each point as a Gaussian distribution, improving robustness
to noise but increasing computational cost. \textit{Normal Distributions Transform
(NDT)} \cite{biber2003normal} represents the environment as a set of Gaussian
distributions on a voxel grid and aligns scans by maximising the likelihood of
observed points. Takeuchi and Tsubouchi \cite{takeuchi2006scan} extended NDT to 3D,
while Akai~\textit{et al.} \cite{akai2017robust} incorporated experimentally
determined scan uncertainty to further improve robustness.

Map-matching approaches represent a complementary strategy. Levinson and
Thrun \cite{levinson2010robust} demonstrated precise urban localisation by matching
intensity measurements against a pre-built probabilistic map. Wolcott and
Eustice \cite{wolcott2017robust} employed multiresolution Gaussian mixture models,
achieving sub-metre accuracy across diverse weather conditions. Burgard
\textit{et al.} \cite{burgard2008map} proposed a map-based framework tailored
to urban environments by exploiting permanent structural features. Although effective,
all map-based methods require costly offline map construction and are vulnerable to
map staleness in dynamic environments.

Yoneda \textit{et al.} \cite{yoneda2014lidar} demonstrated that matching salient
scan features against a high-precision 3D map yields accurate localisation but noted
that performance degrades in feature-sparse scenarios such as open highways.
Im~\textit{et al.} \cite{im2016vertical,im2018extended} proposed vertical corner
and line map representations to reduce map size while maintaining localisation
accuracy in urban areas. Li~\textit{et al.} \cite{li2018hybrid} introduced a hybrid
filtering framework that combines particle filtering with LiDAR scan matching,
improving robustness in industrial environments with repetitive structure.

In contrast to these methods, the proposed framework requires no pre-built map,
no initial pose estimate, and no iterative scan registration, instead extracting
position directly from the spatial statistics of a single point cloud frame.

% ---------------------------
\subsection{GPS and Inertial Navigation}

GPS remains the dominant positioning technology for outdoor vehicles due to its
global coverage and ease of integration \cite{luo2019localization}. However,
multipath effects and signal blockage in urban environments, tunnels, and under
dense tree cover severely degrade GPS accuracy \cite{schreiber2016vehicle}.
Inertial Navigation Systems (INS), based on accelerometers and gyroscopes, can
bridge short GPS outages through dead-reckoning but suffer from unbounded drift
accumulation over time \cite{wang2016accurate}. Tightly-coupled GPS/INS fusion
partially mitigates this by continuously correcting inertial drift, yet the
fundamental dependence on satellite availability persists.

Aldibaja \textit{et al.} \cite{aldibaja2017robust} demonstrated intensity-based
LiDAR localisation as a GPS substitute on snow-wet road surfaces, highlighting
LiDAR's particular advantage in adverse weather and motivating the GPS-free
philosophy adopted in this paper.

% ---------------------------
\subsection{Radar and Vision-Based Approaches}

Radar-based localisation has attracted growing interest due to its robustness in
adverse weather. Ward and Folkesson \cite{ward2016vehicle} demonstrated localisation
using low-cost automotive radar, while Gwon~\textit{et al.} \cite{gwon2017generation}
generated lane-level road maps suitable for radar-based guidance. However, radar
provides significantly lower spatial resolution than LiDAR, limiting its ability to
resolve fine environmental structure.

Vision-based localisation leverages the rich semantic content of camera images for
place recognition, lane detection, and landmark matching \cite{sun2006on}. While
camera systems offer high resolution at low cost, their performance is highly sensitive
to illumination changes, lens soiling, and adverse weather---conditions under which
LiDAR continues to operate reliably.

% ---------------------------
\subsection{Point Cloud Filtering and Feature Extraction}

Preprocessing raw LiDAR data is a critical step in any localisation pipeline.
The Savitzky-Golay filter \cite{savitzky1964smoothing}, originally developed for
analytical chemistry, has been adopted in robotics and sensor signal processing for
its ability to smooth discrete noisy sequences while preserving relative maxima,
minima, and overall signal shape---properties essential for maintaining the geometric
fidelity of LiDAR returns. Crucially, the selection of window length and polynomial
order governs the trade-off between noise suppression and feature preservation; fixed
parameters inevitably mismatch the noise characteristics of individual frames in
dynamic driving scenarios. The proposed method addresses this by dynamically
optimising these parameters on a per-frame basis using mean squared error minimisation.
