% ABSTRACT

\begin{abstract}
Accurate localisation is a fundamental requirement for the safe and reliable operation of
autonomous vehicles (AVs), yet Global Positioning System (GPS) signals remain
unreliable or entirely unavailable in urban canyons, tunnels, and densely forested areas.
This paper presents a novel LiDAR-based localisation framework that operates
independently of GPS by exploiting the rich spatial structure of three-dimensional point
cloud data. The proposed method integrates three complementary components: dynamic
Savitzky-Golay parameter adjustment for adaptive noise suppression, first- and
second-order derivative analysis of polar-transformed point clouds for environmental
feature extraction, and a weighted angular-distribution sensor position estimator.
Extensive experiments are conducted on the KITTI-CARLA benchmark dataset spanning
seven simulated environments that cover urban, rural, and highway scenarios. The
proposed approach is compared against four established baseline methods: K-Means
clustering, Convex Hull estimation, Minimum Enclosing Circle, and Iterative Closest
Point (ICP). Results demonstrate consistent improvements of 57\%--96\% in localisation
accuracy across all environments, with average positional errors as low as 2.46~m,
confirming the robustness and generalisability of the framework.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{A}{utonomous} vehicles (AVs) represent one of the most transformative
developments in modern transportation, promising to reduce road fatalities, alleviate
traffic congestion, and improve mobility for those unable to drive \cite{hussain2019autonomous}.
From simple driver-assistance features such as adaptive cruise control and lane-keeping
to the conditional automation defined at SAE Level~3 \cite{sae2021j3016}, the field has
advanced rapidly over the past decade. Central to this progress is the ability of an AV
to determine its precise position and orientation within its environment---a capability
known as \emph{localisation}. Without accurate and reliable localisation, even the most
sophisticated perception and planning algorithms cannot guarantee safe navigation.

Contemporary localisation systems rely heavily on the Global Positioning System (GPS),
which provides metre-level accuracy under open-sky conditions
\cite{luo2019localization}. However, GPS performance degrades significantly in
environments where satellite signals are obstructed or reflected, including urban
canyons formed by tall buildings, road tunnels, underground car parks, and densely
forested rural roads \cite{schreiber2016vehicle}. In these GPS-denied or GPS-degraded
scenarios, AVs must depend on onboard sensors to maintain continuous positional
awareness.

Light Detection and Ranging (LiDAR) sensors have emerged as a leading technology for
GPS-independent localisation owing to their ability to generate dense, high-resolution
three-dimensional (3D) point clouds at high frame rates \cite{kocic2018sensors}.
Unlike cameras, LiDAR is not affected by ambient lighting conditions, and unlike Radar,
it provides centimetre-level spatial resolution, making it well suited for detailed
environmental mapping and feature extraction. Despite these advantages, extracting
reliable positional estimates from raw LiDAR point clouds in diverse, unstructured
environments remains a challenging open problem.

\subsection{Limitations of Existing Approaches}

Established LiDAR localisation methods can be broadly categorised into
\emph{geometric registration} approaches and \emph{map-based matching} approaches.
Geometric registration methods such as Iterative Closest Point (ICP)
\cite{besl1992method}, Generalised-ICP (G-ICP) \cite{segal2009generalized}, and
Normal Distributions Transform (NDT) \cite{biber2003normal} align successive point
clouds to estimate relative motion. While effective in feature-rich environments, these
methods are sensitive to initial alignment quality, prone to local minima, and
computationally demanding for real-time deployment. Map-based methods \cite{levinson2010robust,wolcott2017robust} achieve high accuracy but require pre-built
high-definition (HD) maps that are costly to construct and maintain, and which quickly
become outdated in dynamic environments.

Simpler geometric estimators---including K-Means centroid clustering, Convex Hull
approximation, and Minimum Enclosing Circle fitting---offer lower computational cost
but oversimplify the spatial distribution of point cloud data. These methods frequently
fail to capture fine-grained environmental structure, leading to large positional errors
in scenarios involving tight turns, path overlaps, or varying point cloud density.

\subsection{Contributions}

This paper addresses these limitations by proposing a novel LiDAR-only localisation
framework that does not require HD maps, prior scene knowledge, or GPS assistance. The
framework processes raw LiDAR point cloud data through a three-stage pipeline that
adaptively suppresses noise, extracts meaningful environmental boundaries, and
triangulates sensor position using angular feature density. The specific contributions
of this work are as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Dynamic Savitzky-Golay Adaptive Filter:} A parameter-free smoothing
    stage that automatically selects the optimal window length and polynomial order for
    the Savitzky-Golay filter \cite{savitzky1964smoothing} based on the noise
    characteristics of each individual point cloud frame, preserving critical structural
    features while suppressing measurement noise.

  \item \textbf{Polar-Domain Derivative Analysis:} A novel application of first- and
    second-order derivatives to polar-transformed LiDAR data that identifies
    significant environmental boundaries---such as edges, corners, and surface
    discontinuities---which serve as reliable localisation anchors across diverse
    scene types.

  \item \textbf{Weighted Angular Position Estimator:} A sensor position estimation
    function that refines an initial geometric-centroid estimate by weighting
    contributions from angularly dense regions of the point cloud and further
    correcting the estimate using derivative-identified feature locations.

  \item \textbf{Comprehensive Experimental Evaluation:} Systematic evaluation on the
    KITTI-CARLA benchmark dataset across seven distinct simulated environments,
    demonstrating improvements of 57\%--96\% in localisation accuracy over four
    baseline methods: K-Means, Convex Hull, Minimum Enclosing Circle, and ICP.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Paper Organisation}

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related}
reviews related work in LiDAR-based localisation. Section~\ref{sec:dataset}
describes the KITTI-CARLA dataset and the baseline localisation methods used for
comparison. Section~\ref{sec:method} presents the proposed framework in detail.
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports experimental results and analysis.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes the paper and outlines directions for future
research.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

Localisation for autonomous vehicles has been studied extensively across a range of
sensor modalities. This section reviews the most relevant prior work, organised by
sensing approach, and identifies the gaps that motivate the proposed method.

% ---------------------------
\subsection{LiDAR-Based Localisation}

LiDAR sensors have been a cornerstone of autonomous vehicle research since the
DARPA Urban Challenge \cite{hussain2019autonomous}, owing to their ability to
generate dense, geometrically accurate 3D point clouds independent of lighting
conditions. The most widely adopted LiDAR localisation algorithms are built on
point-cloud registration.

\textit{Iterative Closest Point (ICP)} \cite{besl1992method} estimates the rigid
transformation between successive scans by iteratively minimising the sum of
squared distances between corresponding points. Although accurate in
feature-rich environments, ICP is susceptible to local minima, sensitive to
the quality of the initial alignment estimate, and computationally expensive
for dense point clouds. \textit{Generalised-ICP (G-ICP)} \cite{segal2009generalized}
extends ICP by modelling each point as a Gaussian distribution, improving robustness
to noise but increasing computational cost. \textit{Normal Distributions Transform
(NDT)} \cite{biber2003normal} represents the environment as a set of Gaussian
distributions on a voxel grid and aligns scans by maximising the likelihood of
observed points. Takeuchi and Tsubouchi \cite{takeuchi2006scan} extended NDT to 3D,
while Akai~\textit{et al.} \cite{akai2017robust} incorporated experimentally
determined scan uncertainty to further improve robustness.

Map-matching approaches represent a complementary strategy. Levinson and
Thrun \cite{levinson2010robust} demonstrated precise urban localisation by matching
intensity measurements against a pre-built probabilistic map. Wolcott and
Eustice \cite{wolcott2017robust} employed multiresolution Gaussian mixture models,
achieving sub-metre accuracy across diverse weather conditions. Burgard
\textit{et al.} \cite{burgard2008map} proposed a map-based framework tailored
to urban environments by exploiting permanent structural features. Although effective,
all map-based methods require costly offline map construction and are vulnerable to
map staleness in dynamic environments.

Yoneda \textit{et al.} \cite{yoneda2014lidar} demonstrated that matching salient
scan features against a high-precision 3D map yields accurate localisation but noted
that performance degrades in feature-sparse scenarios such as open highways.
Im~\textit{et al.} \cite{im2016vertical,im2018extended} proposed vertical corner
and line map representations to reduce map size while maintaining localisation
accuracy in urban areas. Li~\textit{et al.} \cite{li2018hybrid} introduced a hybrid
filtering framework that combines particle filtering with LiDAR scan matching,
improving robustness in industrial environments with repetitive structure.

In contrast to these methods, the proposed framework requires no pre-built map,
no initial pose estimate, and no iterative scan registration, instead extracting
position directly from the spatial statistics of a single point cloud frame.

% ---------------------------
\subsection{GPS and Inertial Navigation}

GPS remains the dominant positioning technology for outdoor vehicles due to its
global coverage and ease of integration \cite{luo2019localization}. However,
multipath effects and signal blockage in urban environments, tunnels, and under
dense tree cover severely degrade GPS accuracy \cite{schreiber2016vehicle}.
Inertial Navigation Systems (INS), based on accelerometers and gyroscopes, can
bridge short GPS outages through dead-reckoning but suffer from unbounded drift
accumulation over time \cite{wang2016accurate}. Tightly-coupled GPS/INS fusion
partially mitigates this by continuously correcting inertial drift, yet the
fundamental dependence on satellite availability persists.

Aldibaja \textit{et al.} \cite{aldibaja2017robust} demonstrated intensity-based
LiDAR localisation as a GPS substitute on snow-wet road surfaces, highlighting
LiDAR's particular advantage in adverse weather and motivating the GPS-free
philosophy adopted in this paper.

% ---------------------------
\subsection{Radar and Vision-Based Approaches}

Radar-based localisation has attracted growing interest due to its robustness in
adverse weather. Ward and Folkesson \cite{ward2016vehicle} demonstrated localisation
using low-cost automotive radar, while Gwon~\textit{et al.} \cite{gwon2017generation}
generated lane-level road maps suitable for radar-based guidance. However, radar
provides significantly lower spatial resolution than LiDAR, limiting its ability to
resolve fine environmental structure.

Vision-based localisation leverages the rich semantic content of camera images for
place recognition, lane detection, and landmark matching \cite{sun2006on}. While
camera systems offer high resolution at low cost, their performance is highly sensitive
to illumination changes, lens soiling, and adverse weather---conditions under which
LiDAR continues to operate reliably.

% ---------------------------
\subsection{Point Cloud Filtering and Feature Extraction}

Preprocessing raw LiDAR data is a critical step in any localisation pipeline.
The Savitzky-Golay filter \cite{savitzky1964smoothing}, originally developed for
analytical chemistry, has been adopted in robotics and sensor signal processing for
its ability to smooth discrete noisy sequences while preserving relative maxima,
minima, and overall signal shape---properties essential for maintaining the geometric
fidelity of LiDAR returns. Crucially, the selection of window length and polynomial
order governs the trade-off between noise suppression and feature preservation; fixed
parameters inevitably mismatch the noise characteristics of individual frames in
dynamic driving scenarios. The proposed method addresses this by dynamically
optimising these parameters on a per-frame basis using mean squared error minimisation.

\section{Dataset and Baseline Methods}\label{sec:dataset}

This section describes the benchmark dataset used for evaluation and provides
a concise formulation of the four baseline localisation methods against which
the proposed framework is compared.

% ---------------------------
\subsection{The KITTI-CARLA Dataset}

Experiments are conducted on the \textit{KITTI-CARLA} dataset
\cite{geiger2012kitti, dosovitskiy2017carla}, a synthetic benchmark that combines
the data format and annotation conventions of the KITTI vision benchmark
\cite{geiger2012kitti} with scenes generated by the CARLA open-source urban
driving simulator \cite{dosovitskiy2017carla}. CARLA provides physically realistic
sensor models, including a 64-beam LiDAR sensor that generates dense point clouds
at 10~Hz, and produces ground-truth pose information with millimetre-level
precision---enabling rigorous quantitative evaluation of localisation accuracy.

The dataset spans \textbf{seven distinct environments} (Town~01 through Town~07),
each representing a different type of driving scenario:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{Towns~01--03}: Urban environments with multi-lane roads,
    intersections, roundabouts, and dense roadside infrastructure.
  \item \textit{Towns~04--05}: Mixed environments combining urban districts
    with open suburban roads and varying building density.
  \item \textit{Towns~06--07}: Rural and highway-style environments with
    fewer structural landmarks, wider lane separations, and reduced point
    cloud density.
\end{itemize}

Each town provides a sequence of LiDAR point cloud frames stored in PLY format,
paired with ground-truth sensor poses expressed as $(x, y)$ coordinates in a
local Cartesian reference frame. The diversity of environments---in terms of
structural density, road curvature, and point cloud characteristics---ensures
that evaluation results are not biased towards any single scene type. A
visualisation of the ground-truth trajectories for all seven towns is shown
in Fig.~\ref{fig:groundtruth}.

\begin{figure}[!t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_groundtruth}
  \caption{Ground-truth sensor trajectories for Towns~01--07 of the
    KITTI-CARLA dataset. Each town exhibits distinct path geometry,
    ranging from closed urban loops (Towns~01--03) to open highway

  \label{fig:groundtruth}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Data Preprocessing}

Prior to applying any localisation method, each raw PLY point cloud undergoes
two preprocessing steps common to all methods evaluated in this work.

\textit{Outlier removal:} Statistical outlier filtering is applied using an
Isolation Forest model to identify and discard points whose Euclidean distance
from the local neighbourhood mean exceeds three standard deviations. This step
removes spurious returns caused by dust particles, sensor noise, and distant
reflections that carry no useful structural information.

\textit{Coordinate projection:} The 3D point cloud $(x, y, z)$ is projected
onto the horizontal plane by discarding the $z$-component, yielding a 2D
point set $\mathcal{P} = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$ suitable for planar
localisation. This is consistent with standard practice in urban driving,
where lateral positioning is the primary concern.

% ---------------------------
\subsection{Baseline Localisation Methods}

Four geometric baseline methods are evaluated. All operate on the same
preprocessed 2D point set $\mathcal{P}$ described above and produce a
scalar position estimate $\hat{\mathbf{p}} = (\hat{x}, \hat{y})$.

\subsubsection{K-Means Centroid (K-Means Path)}

K-Means clustering \cite{kocic2018sensors} partitions $\mathcal{P}$ into
$k$ clusters by iteratively minimising the within-cluster sum of squared
Euclidean distances. The sensor position is estimated as the centroid of
the largest cluster, on the assumption that the densest spatial cluster
corresponds to the region nearest the sensor:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\mathbf{p}}_{\text{KM}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}^*|}
  \sum_{(x_i,y_i) \in \mathcal{C}^*} (x_i,\, y_i),
  \label{eq:kmeans}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{C}^*$ denotes the largest cluster. In this study $k{=}3$
is used, chosen empirically to balance cluster granularity and stability.
K-Means offers low computational cost but is sensitive to initialisation
and to the uneven spatial distribution of LiDAR returns, particularly
in open environments.

\subsubsection{Convex Hull Centroid (Convex Hull Path)}

The convex hull of $\mathcal{P}$ is the smallest convex polygon enclosing
all points. The sensor position is estimated as the centroid of this polygon:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\mathbf{p}}_{\text{CH}} = \frac{1}{M}
  \sum_{j=1}^{M} \mathbf{v}_j,
  \label{eq:convexhull}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_M$ are the $M$ vertices of the
convex hull. While robust to noise by construction, the convex hull
oversimplifies complex, non-convex environments and is insensitive to the
internal distribution of points, leading to systematic bias in scenarios
with asymmetric point density.

\subsubsection{Minimum Enclosing Circle (Minimum Circle Path)}

The minimum enclosing circle (MEC) is the smallest circle that contains all
points in $\mathcal{P}$. The sensor position is taken as its centre
$\hat{\mathbf{p}}_{\text{MEC}} = (c_x, c_y)$, computed via Welzl's
algorithm in expected $O(N)$ time. Like the convex hull, the MEC is
dominated by the most distant outlier points and fails to exploit the
density distribution of returns, producing estimates that mirror the
convex hull errors in curvature-rich scenes.

\subsubsection{Iterative Closest Point (ICP Path)}

ICP \cite{besl1992method} estimates the rigid transformation
$(\mathbf{R}, \mathbf{t})$ that aligns the current point cloud
$\mathcal{P}_t$ to a reference cloud $\mathcal{P}_{t-1}$ by minimising:
\begin{equation}
  E(\mathbf{R}, \mathbf{t}) =
  \sum_{i} \bigl\| \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{p}_i^{(t)} + \mathbf{t}
  - \mathbf{q}_i^{(t-1)} \bigr\|^2,
  \label{eq:icp}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{q}_i^{(t-1)}$ is the closest point in $\mathcal{P}_{t-1}$ to the
transformed point $\mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{p}_i^{(t)} + \mathbf{t}$. The accumulated
translation vector is used to update the global position estimate. ICP is the most
sophisticated of the four baselines but is the most sensitive to initialisation
quality: a poor initial alignment causes convergence to a local minimum, resulting
in large positional errors that propagate across frames.

\subsection{Evaluation Metric}

All methods are evaluated using the \textit{mean Euclidean error} (MEE) between
the estimated sensor position $\hat{\mathbf{p}}$ and the ground-truth position
$\mathbf{p}^*$ across all $T$ frames in a town sequence:
\begin{equation}
  \text{MEE} = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T}
  \bigl\| \hat{\mathbf{p}}_t - \mathbf{p}^*_t \bigr\|_2.
  \label{eq:mee}
\end{equation}
The percentage improvement of the proposed method over a baseline $b$ is then
defined as:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_b = \frac{\text{MEE}_b - \text{MEE}_{\text{proposed}}}
                  {\text{MEE}_b} \times 100\%.
  \label{eq:improvement}
\end{equation}

\section{Proposed Method}\label{sec:method}

The proposed localisation framework processes a single preprocessed 2D point cloud
$\mathcal{P} = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$ through three sequential stages:
(i)~adaptive noise suppression via a dynamically parameterised Savitzky-Golay
filter, (ii)~environmental feature extraction via polar-domain derivative analysis,
and (iii)~weighted angular position estimation. Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline} illustrates
the complete pipeline.

\begin{figure}[!t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig_pipeline}
  \caption{Overview of the proposed three-stage localisation pipeline. A raw LiDAR
    point cloud is converted to polar coordinates, adaptively smoothed, differentiated
    to identify environmental features, and finally used to produce a refined sensor
    position estimate.}
  \label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

% ---------------------------
\subsection{Stage 1: Polar Coordinate Transformation}

The Cartesian point set $\mathcal{P}$ is first converted to a polar representation
centred on the geometric mean $\bar{\mathbf{p}} = (\bar{x}, \bar{y})$:
\begin{equation}
  r_i = \sqrt{(x_i - \bar{x})^2 + (y_i - \bar{y})^2}, \quad
  \theta_i = \operatorname{atan2}(y_i - \bar{y},\; x_i - \bar{x}),
  \label{eq:polar}
\end{equation}
yielding the polar set $\mathcal{Q} = \{(r_i, \theta_i)\}_{i=1}^{N}$.
The data is then sorted by $\theta_i$ in ascending order to produce a
continuously ordered angular sequence, which is a prerequisite for
meaningful derivative computation in the next stage.

% ---------------------------
\subsection{Stage 2: Dynamic Savitzky-Golay Adaptive Smoothing}

Raw LiDAR returns contain measurement noise that, if uncorrected, causes spurious
extrema in derivative signals and corrupts feature detection. The Savitzky-Golay
filter \cite{savitzky1964smoothing} smooths a discrete sequence by fitting
overlapping polynomial windows via least squares, preserving signal shape
characteristics such as local maxima and minima. For a window of length $w$
and polynomial order $d$, the smoothed value at position $i$ is:
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{s}_i = \sum_{j=-m}^{m} c_j\, s_{i+j}, \qquad m = \tfrac{w-1}{2},
  \label{eq:savgol}
\end{equation}
where $c_j$ are the Savitzky-Golay convolution coefficients determined by the
least-squares polynomial fit and $s_i \in \{r_i, \theta_i\}$.

\textbf{Dynamic parameter selection.} The key limitation of the standard
Savitzky-Golay filter is that the window length $w$ and polynomial order $d$
must be chosen in advance. Fixed parameters that suit one frame may over-smooth
structural features or under-suppress noise in another. To address this, we
introduce an automatic parameter optimisation procedure that selects $w^*$ and
$d^*$ independently for each frame by minimising the mean squared error (MSE)
between the smoothed signal and the original:
\begin{equation}
  (w^*, d^*) = \underset{w \in \mathcal{W},\; d \in \{2,3,4\}}{\arg\min}\;
  \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \bigl(\tilde{s}_i^{(w,d)} - s_i\bigr)^2,
  \label{eq:dynparam}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{W} = \{11, 13, 15, \ldots, w_{\max}\}$ is the set of odd window
lengths up to a maximum $w_{\max}$ (set to $\min(51, N)$ in practice to avoid
over-smoothing small point clouds). This optimisation is performed separately
for both the radial sequence $\{r_i\}$ and the angular sequence $\{\theta_i\}$,
yielding two independently tuned filters. Algorithm~\ref{alg:dynsg} summarises
the procedure.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{Dynamic Savitzky-Golay Parameter Selection}
\label{alg:dynsg}
\begin{algorithmic}
\STATE \textbf{Input:} signal $\{s_i\}_{i=1}^{N}$, max window $w_{\max}$
\STATE \textbf{Output:} optimal window $w^*$, polynomial order $d^*$
\STATE $\text{best\_MSE} \leftarrow \infty$
\FOR{each odd $w \in \{11, 13, \ldots, w_{\max}\}$}
  \FOR{$d \in \{2, 3, 4\}$}
    \STATE Apply Savitzky-Golay filter: $\tilde{s}_i \leftarrow \text{SG}(s, w, d)$
    \STATE $\text{MSE} \leftarrow \frac{1}{N}\sum_i (\tilde{s}_i - s_i)^2$
    \IF{$\text{MSE} < \text{best\_MSE}$}
      \STATE $\text{best\_MSE} \leftarrow \text{MSE};\quad w^* \leftarrow w;\quad d^* \leftarrow d$
    \ENDIF
  \ENDFOR
\ENDFOR
\RETURN $w^*,\; d^*$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

% ---------------------------
\subsection{Stage 3: Polar-Domain Derivative Analysis}

With the smoothed radial and angular sequences $\{\tilde{r}_i\}$ and
$\{\tilde{\theta}_i\}$ in hand, we compute first- and second-order finite
differences to locate regions of significant environmental change.

\subsubsection{First-Order Derivatives}

The first-order derivatives quantify the rate of change of range and angle
with respect to the sorted sample index $i$:
\begin{equation}
  r'_i = \frac{\tilde{r}_{i+1} - \tilde{r}_{i-1}}{2}, \qquad
  \theta'_i = \frac{\tilde{\theta}_{i+1} - \tilde{\theta}_{i-1}}{2}.
  \label{eq:first_deriv}
\end{equation}
Large magnitudes in $|r'_i|$ indicate abrupt changes in measured range,
corresponding to surface discontinuities such as building edges, kerbs, and
vegetation boundaries.

\subsubsection{Second-Order Derivatives}

The second-order derivatives detect inflection points and regions of rapidly
accelerating change, which correspond to corners and concave features:
\begin{equation}
  r''_i = \tilde{r}_{i+1} - 2\tilde{r}_i + \tilde{r}_{i-1}, \qquad
  \theta''_i = \tilde{\theta}_{i+1} - 2\tilde{\theta}_{i} + \tilde{\theta}_{i-1}.
  \label{eq:second_deriv}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Angular Wrap-Around Correction}

Because $\theta \in (-\pi, \pi]$ is a cyclic variable, naive finite differences
produce spurious large values at the $\pm\pi$ boundary. A correction is applied
wherever $|\theta'_i| > \pi$:
\begin{equation}
  \theta'_i \leftarrow \theta'_i - 2\pi \cdot \operatorname{sign}(\theta'_i),
  \label{eq:wraparound}
\end{equation}
ensuring derivative continuity across the angular wrap-around.

% ---------------------------
\subsection{Stage 4: Weighted Angular Position Estimation}

The final stage fuses the geometric centre, the angular density distribution,
and the derivative feature map into a refined sensor position estimate through
three successive refinement steps.

\textbf{Step 1 — Geometric centroid initialisation.}
An initial position estimate is computed as the mean of all point coordinates:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\mathbf{p}}^{(0)} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} (x_i, y_i).
  \label{eq:centroid}
\end{equation}

\textbf{Step 2 — Angular density refinement.}
The angular distribution $\{\theta_i\}$ is discretised into a histogram
$H(\phi)$ with bin width $\Delta\phi = 5^{\circ}$. Peaks in $H(\phi)$ identify
angular directions where point cloud returns are most densely concentrated,
corresponding to nearby planar surfaces such as walls or building facades.
The position estimate is shifted towards the weighted mean of these dominant
angular directions:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\mathbf{p}}^{(1)} = \hat{\mathbf{p}}^{(0)} +
  \alpha \sum_{k \in \mathcal{K}}
  H(\phi_k) \begin{pmatrix} \cos\phi_k \\ \sin\phi_k \end{pmatrix},
  \label{eq:angularshift}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{K}$ is the set of peak bin indices and $\alpha$ is a
normalisation constant scaled by the total point count.

\textbf{Step 3 — Derivative-based feature correction.}
Points with high combined derivative magnitude:
\begin{equation}
  D_i = |r'_i| + |r''_i| + |\theta'_i| + |\theta''_i|
  \label{eq:deriv_magnitude}
\end{equation}
are identified as environmental feature anchors (edges, corners,
discontinuities). The top $\kappa = 10\%$ of points ranked by $D_i$ form
the feature set $\mathcal{F}$. The final estimate is computed as the
weighted centroid of $\mathcal{F}$, with weights proportional to $D_i$:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\mathbf{p}}^{(2)} = \frac{\sum_{i \in \mathcal{F}} D_i\, (x_i, y_i)}
                                 {\sum_{i \in \mathcal{F}} D_i}.
  \label{eq:featurecentroid}
\end{equation}
The output $\hat{\mathbf{p}}^{(2)}$ is the final sensor position estimate
returned by the framework.

% ---------------------------
\subsection{Computational Complexity}

The dominant cost is the dynamic parameter search in Stage~2. For a point cloud
of $N$ points, $|\mathcal{W}|$ candidate window lengths, and three polynomial
orders, the complexity is $O(|\mathcal{W}| \cdot N)$ per signal. Sorting in
Stage~1 costs $O(N \log N)$, and all derivative and estimation steps are $O(N)$.
The total per-frame complexity is therefore $O(|\mathcal{W}| \cdot N)$, which is
linear in $N$ for a fixed search grid---substantially cheaper than the iterative
$O(N^2)$ worst-case cost of ICP.

\subsection{Analysis by Baseline Method}

\subsubsection{K-Means Path}
K-Means produced the lowest baseline errors among the four methods, with MEE
ranging from 7.51~m (Town~03) to 11.60~m (Town~01), reflecting its ability to
broadly capture dominant spatial clusters in the point cloud. However, K-Means
is inherently sensitive to cluster initialisation and to uneven point density
caused by partial occlusions. The proposed method improved upon K-Means by
57.0\%--72.4\% across towns, with the smallest gains in feature-sparse rural
environments (Town~05: 57.0\%) and the largest in mixed and structured urban
scenes (Town~04: 72.4\%). These gains arise because the derivative analysis
extracts sub-cluster boundary structure that K-Means centroids cannot resolve.

\subsubsection{Convex Hull and Minimum Circle Paths}
The Convex Hull and Minimum Enclosing Circle methods produced identical MEE
values in every town, a consequence of both methods being dominated by the
same set of outermost points in the 2D projection. MEE ranged from 23.16~m
to 32.18~m, consistently three to four times worse than K-Means. Both methods
fail to exploit the internal spatial distribution of the point cloud, reducing
the localisation problem to a geometric bounding operation that is highly
sensitive to outlier points near the scan periphery. The proposed method
improved upon these methods by 86.8\%--90.4\%, the most consistent improvement
band observed across any baseline.

\subsubsection{ICP Path}
ICP produced the largest errors of all baselines, with MEE ranging from
58.27~m (Town~05) to 75.32~m (Town~06), representing roughly 6--8$\times$ the
error of the proposed method. The catastrophic failure of ICP in this
setting is attributable to the absence of a reliable initial alignment: without
a prior pose estimate, ICP frequently converges to incorrect local minima and
the accumulated registration errors compound rapidly across frames. The
proposed method improved over ICP by 94.1\%--96.3\%, the highest improvement
margins observed, confirming that single-frame derivative-based estimation is
far more stable than multi-frame registration in GPS-denied cold-start
scenarios.

% ---------------------------
\subsection{Cross-Environment Consistency}

A notable characteristic of the proposed method is its consistent performance
across environments of very different character. The MEE varies only between
2.46~m (Town~07, rural/highway) and 3.61~m (Town~01, dense urban), a range
of just 1.15~m despite the substantial differences in structural density and
road geometry between these environments. This contrasts with the baselines,
which exhibit much wider variance: K-Means MEE spans 4.09~m and ICP MEE
spans 17.05~m across the same towns.

The relatively lower error in rural and highway towns (06--07) compared to
dense urban towns (01--03) reflects the effect of sparser, more regular
point cloud structure: fewer overlapping returns from nearby buildings means
that derivative peaks more reliably identify genuine environmental boundaries
rather than cluttered near-field returns.

baselines and the proposed method overlaid on the ground-truth path for
representative Towns~01 and~07. The proposed method tracks the ground truth
closely throughout, while ICP diverges almost immediately and the geometric
methods (Convex Hull, Minimum Circle) produce trajectories offset by tens

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}\label{sec:conclusion}

% ---------------------------
\subsection{Conclusion}

This paper presented a novel GPS-independent LiDAR localisation framework for
autonomous vehicles, designed to operate reliably in environments where satellite
positioning is unreliable or entirely unavailable. The framework processes a
single 3D LiDAR point cloud frame through a four-stage pipeline: polar coordinate
transformation, dynamic Savitzky-Golay adaptive smoothing, first- and
second-order polar-domain derivative analysis, and weighted angular position
estimation. Unlike map-based or scan-registration methods, the proposed approach
requires no pre-built HD map, no initial pose estimate, and no inter-frame
correspondence, making it suitable for cold-start deployment in previously
unseen environments.

Comprehensive evaluation on the KITTI-CARLA benchmark across seven
environments---spanning dense urban, mixed suburban, and rural/highway
scenarios---demonstrated consistent and substantial improvements over four
established baselines. The proposed method achieved a mean Euclidean error of
3.06~m averaged across all towns, representing improvements of 57\%--72\% over
K-Means clustering, 87\%--90\% over Convex Hull and Minimum Enclosing Circle
estimation, and 94\%--96\% over Iterative Closest Point registration. Critically,
the method maintained low and stable per-frame errors throughout each town
sequence, in contrast to ICP which suffered severe accumulated drift and the
geometric bounding methods which exhibited persistent systematic bias.

The dynamic Savitzky-Golay parameter selection proved particularly effective at
adapting the noise suppression level to the varying density and noise
characteristics of individual frames, while the derivative-based feature
identification reliably located structural boundaries---edges, corners, and
surface discontinuities---that serve as robust localisation anchors across
diverse scene types.

These results establish the proposed framework as a practical, computationally
efficient foundation for GPS-denied localisation in autonomous vehicles, with
relevance extending to mobile robotics, unmanned aerial vehicles, and
infrastructure-free mapping systems.

% ---------------------------
\subsection{Limitations}

The current work has several limitations that bound the scope of the results.
First, all experiments are conducted on simulated data from the KITTI-CARLA
dataset. Although CARLA provides high-fidelity sensor models, the gap between
simulated and real sensor noise, weather effects, and dynamic object interference
remains to be characterised. Second, the framework currently operates in 2D by
projecting the point cloud onto the horizontal plane; tall or multi-level
structures whose vertical extent is informative for localisation are therefore
not exploited. Third, the method produces absolute position estimates in a local
coordinate frame but does not accumulate these estimates into a globally
consistent trajectory, meaning long-term drift correction is not addressed.

% ---------------------------
\subsection{Future Work}

Several directions offer promising opportunities to extend this work.

\textbf{Real-world validation.} The most immediate priority is evaluation on
real LiDAR datasets such as the original KITTI odometry benchmark
\cite{geiger2012kitti} and the nuScenes dataset, which include real sensor
noise, dynamic agents, and varying weather conditions. Such evaluation will
reveal whether the performance gains observed in simulation transfer to
practical deployment.

\textbf{3D extension.} Extending the derivative analysis to the full 3D point
cloud by operating in spherical coordinates $(r, \theta, \phi)$ would allow
the framework to exploit vertical structural information---particularly useful
in multi-storey urban environments and underground car parks where horizontal
projections are ambiguous.

\textbf{Temporal fusion and trajectory estimation.} Integrating successive
single-frame position estimates via a Kalman filter or particle filter would
suppress frame-to-frame noise and enable globally consistent trajectory
estimation. Coupling this with wheel odometry or IMU data would further
improve robustness during featureless stretches such as long straight
highways.

\textbf{Sensor fusion.} Fusing LiDAR-derived position estimates with
camera-based semantic landmarks, radar returns, and GPS when available
would create a resilient multi-modal localisation system that degrades
gracefully as individual sensors fail or become unreliable.

\textbf{Deep learning integration.} Learned feature detectors trained on
large-scale LiDAR datasets could replace the hand-crafted derivative-based
feature extractor, potentially improving robustness to sensor noise and
point cloud sparsity. A hybrid approach---using learned features for
initial coarse localisation and derivative analysis for refinement---is
a particularly promising direction.
